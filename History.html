Pillars of Alpinism

Witness the greatest moments in mountaineering history.

1786

First Ascent of Mont Blanc

The first ascent of Mont Blanc on August 8, 1786, was accomplished by Dr. Michel-Gabriel Paccard and Jacques Balmat, a crystal hunter, marking the birth of modern alpinism. Motivated by a bounty offered by Genevan scientist Horace-Bénédict de Saussure, the two men set out from Chamonix and, after an overnight bivouac, reached the top at 6:23 PM without modern equipment or organized rope teams, carrying instruments like barometers for measurement. Their success shattered the belief that Mont Blanc was unconquerable, and the following year Saussure himself reached the summit to complete his scientific investigation.

Chamonix, France · 4,808 m

1865

The First Ascent of the Matterhorn

On July 14, 1865, Edward Whymper led a seven-man party to the summit of the Matterhorn, the last great Alpine peak to be climbed. The triumph turned to tragedy on the descent when four members of the rope team fell to their deaths. The ascent closed the golden age of alpinism and remains one of the most consequential climbs in the sport's history — a symbol of both achievement and the consequences of ambition on dangerous ground.

Zermatt, Switzerland · 4,478 m

1953

Everest Conquered by Hillary and Tenzing

On May 29, 1953, Edmund Hillary of New Zealand and Tenzing Norgay, a Sherpa of Nepal, became the first climbers confirmed to reach the summit of Mount Everest. Climbing as part of John Hunt's British expedition, they spent roughly fifteen minutes at the top before descending. The news reached the world on the morning of Queen Elizabeth II's coronation, and Tenzing and Hillary became global symbols of exploration, partnership and humility in the mountains.

Himalaya, Nepal / Tibet · 8,849 m

1938

The Eiger North Face Is Finally Climbed

For years the North Face of the Eiger was considered one of the great unsolved problems of alpinism. On July 21–24, 1938, a team of Anderl Heckmair, Ludwig Vörg, Heinrich Harrer and Fritz Kasparek completed the first ascent after four days of hard climbing and deteriorating weather. Their success opened the modern era of serious north-face climbing in the Alps and turned the Eiger into a proving ground that still defines the sport.

Bernese Oberland, Switzerland · 3,967 m

1950

Annapurna: The First 8,000-Metre Summit

On June 3, 1950, Maurice Herzog and Louis Lachenal reached the summit of Annapurna, the first time any 8,000-metre peak had been climbed. The descent nearly killed them — both suffered severe frostbite, and Herzog lost all his fingers and toes. The expedition's account, Annapurna, became one of the most widely read books in mountaineering and brought high-altitude climbing into public view for the first time.

Himalaya, Nepal · 8,091 m

1980

Messner Solos Everest Without Oxygen

On August 20, 1980, Reinhold Messner became the first person to climb Mount Everest solo and without supplemental oxygen. Starting from the North Col during the monsoon, he ascended in a single push and spent three days on the mountain alone. The ascent redefined what was believed physically possible at extreme altitude and remains one of the most remarkable feats in the sport's history.

Himalaya, Tibet · 8,849 m

1954

K2: The Savage Mountain Is Climbed

On July 31, 1954, Lino Lacedelli and Achille Compagnoni reached the summit of K2 as part of Ardito Desio's Italian expedition, ending decades of failed attempts. The climb was controversial — a dispute over the high camp nearly cost Walter Bonatti and Hunza porter Mahdi the supplies they carried — but it confirmed K2's place as the climber's mountain: lower than Everest, far harder, and unforgiving of mistakes.

Karakoram, Pakistan / China · 8,611 m

2017

Alex Honnold Free Solos El Capitan

On June 3, 2017, Alex Honnold became the first person to climb the 900-metre Freerider route on El Capitan in Yosemite without ropes, harness or any form of protection — a free solo. He had rehearsed the route for years and climbed it in just under four hours. The ascent, filmed for the documentary Free Solo, brought rock climbing into the mainstream and stands as the most consequential free solo in history.

Yosemite, USA · 900 m route

About Us

Welcome to Alpine Ascents

Alpine Ascents is a leader in the mountaineering and climbing world. Our mission is simple: help climbers of every level reach their goals through safe, challenging and fun expeditions.

This site brings everything together in one place: the history of the sport, the skills and gear you need, the dangers to respect, and the clubs that can take you to the mountains.

Our guides and contributors have spent years on rock, snow and ice across the world's great ranges, from the Alps and the Andes to the Himalaya and the Karakoram. We pass on what we have learned in plain language, so you can plan your own climbs with confidence.

Whether you are lacing up boots for your first hill day or preparing for a technical alpine route, the site is built to grow with you: start with the basics, then move on to courses, gear systems and the hazards that demand respect.

Essential Skills

Practical knowledge to keep you safe and confident on the mountain.

Beginner

Skills Every Beginner Should Learn

  • Walking on snow with a steady pace: A slow, rhythmic stride saves energy and keeps your footing secure on slippery or uneven snow.
  • Using trekking poles correctly: Poles improve balance, take load off your knees on descents and help you keep a steady rhythm uphill.
  • Packing and carrying a rucksack: Placing heavy items close to your back and essentials within reach keeps the load balanced and comfortable.
  • Staying hydrated and fuelled: Regular drinking and eating keeps your energy up and helps you think clearly in cold, thin air.
  • Layering for warmth and moisture control: Wearing several thin layers lets you add or remove clothing as conditions change and stops sweat from chilling you.
  • Reading a map and using a compass: These tools help you find your position and follow a route when landmarks are hidden or the weather closes in.
  • Basic weather awareness: Watching clouds, wind and temperature changes helps you spot trouble early and decide when to turn back.
  • Essential knots: The figure-eight, bowline and clove hitch are the core knots for tying in, anchoring and securing a rope.

Intermediate

Skills Every Intermediate Climber Should Learn

  • Walking in crampons on snow and ice: Crampons grip hard surfaces, but they need a flat-footed, deliberate step to avoid snagging your clothing or tripping.
  • Ice axe balance and self-arrest: The ice axe gives you support on steep ground and lets you stop a slide by driving it into the slope.
  • Roped travel as a team: Climbers linked by a rope can catch a fall, which relies on good spacing, communication and rope management.
  • Belaying and abseiling: Belaying protects a climber from falling, and abseiling lets you descend steep ground in a controlled way.
  • Building basic anchors in snow and rock: A secure anchor is the foundation of every belay and rescue, so it has to be strong and well placed.
  • Navigating in poor visibility: In fog, snow or darkness you rely on compass bearings, timing and careful route notes rather than what you can see.
  • Route planning and estimating timings: Good planning matches the route to the team's ability and the daylight available, with time to spare.
  • Spotting early signs of altitude sickness: Headache, nausea and unusual tiredness can warn of a serious problem, and recognising them early lets you act before it worsens.

Advanced

Skills Every Advanced Climber Should Learn

  • Leading multi-pitch routes: The leader climbs first, placing protection and making route decisions, which demands strong technique and calm judgement.
  • Technical ice and mixed climbing: Steep ice and rock-and-ice routes require precise tool and crampon placement, and good fitness.
  • Crevasse rescue and rope hauling: Rescue systems use ropes and pulleys to lift a fallen climber out of a crevasse, and the team needs to practise them beforehand.
  • Assessing avalanche risk and snowpack: Reading forecasts, slope angle and snow layers helps you judge whether a slope is safe to cross.
  • Summit or turnaround decisions under pressure: Setting a turnaround time in advance and sticking to it prevents summit fever from putting the team at risk.
  • Managing a team in emergencies: Staying calm, assigning roles and communicating clearly can decide the outcome of an accident.
  • High-altitude acclimatisation strategy: Climbing high and sleeping low, with gradual height gain, gives the body time to adapt to less oxygen.
  • Wilderness first aid and evacuation planning: Climbers need to treat injuries and cold or altitude illness far from help, and to plan how to get someone down safely.

Courses

Training paths from first steps to professional certification.

Foundational

Where Most Climbers Start

  • Basic Mountaineering Course (BMC): 26-28 days of rock, ice and snow craft, ending with a graduation climb.
  • Advanced Mountaineering Course (AMC): 28 days for BMC graduates, preparing climbers for peaks up to 7,000 m.

Skills Courses

Build Technique in the Mountains

  • Mountain Skills Course (MSC): 3.5 days of crampons, ice axe and avalanche awareness for hikers.
  • Alpine Skills Course (ASC): 7 days introducing climbing on snow and ice.
  • Technical Mountaineering Course (TMC): 8 days of leading, ropework and steep ice.

Professional

The Guide's Path

  • IFMGA Mountain Guide Certification: A multi-year training and assessment pathway recognised in over 20 countries.
  • Search and Rescue and Instructor courses: Specialist training for team leaders and teachers.

Sheltering

How to stay warm and protected when the mountain turns.

Tents

Your Base on the Mountain

  • Four-season tents: Strong poles and a sturdy fly resist wind and snow load on exposed camps.
  • Pitching on snow: Deadman anchors, snow stakes and buried bags hold in soft or windy conditions.
  • Ventilation: Managing condensation keeps sleeping bags and clothing dry overnight.

Snow Shelters

Built From the Mountain

  • Snow caves: Dug into a drift, they stay close to freezing even when outside temperatures drop far below.
  • Quinzhees: A piled and packed mound hollowed out — quick to build, but it needs settling time.
  • Igloos: Cut snow blocks form a strong dome, but building one well takes practice.

Emergency Shelter

When Plans Fall Apart

  • Bivy sacks and bothy bags: Lightweight protection that turns a forced night out into a survivable one.
  • Emergency blankets: Reflective sheets trap heat and double as signalling aids.
  • Site choice: Shelter from wind and avalanche runouts matters more than comfort.

Hazards

The dangers every climber has to understand and respect.

Avalanche

Snowpack and Slopes

  • Reading the forecast: Daily bulletins rate danger and highlight the likely trigger and slope aspects.
  • Slope angle: Most slides release between 30° and 45°, so route choice is your main defence.
  • Companion rescue: A transceiver, probe and shovel — and the practice to use them — save lives.

Altitude

Thin Air, Real Risk

  • Acute mountain sickness: Headache, nausea and fatigue are early warnings — stop ascending.
  • HAPE and HACE: Fluid in the lungs or brain are emergencies; descent is the only reliable treatment.
  • Acclimatisation: Climb high, sleep low, and build rest days into the plan.

Weather & Terrain

What the Mountain Throws

  • Storms and whiteouts: Wind, cold and lost visibility strip away judgement — carry navigation you trust.
  • Rockfall and icefall: Move quickly through hazard zones and never linger below seracs.
  • Crevasse falls: Roped travel and glacier skills are non-negotiable on glaciated terrain.

Clubs

Find people to climb with and routes worth training for.

Local Clubs

Learn Close to Home

  • Weekend meets: Club trips are the cheapest way to build mileage with experienced partners.
  • Skills workshops: Many clubs run ropework, navigation and winter-skills sessions for members.
  • Mentoring: Climbing with more experienced members is how most people progress safely.

National Bodies

Training and Representation

  • Federations: National mountaineering bodies run courses, huts and expeditions.
  • Insurance and access: Membership often includes rescue cover and access agreements.
  • Youth programmes: Many federations run subsidised camps for young climbers.

International

Climb Beyond Borders

  • UIAA: The international federation links member clubs and sets safety standards.
  • Alpine clubs: Historic clubs such as the Alpine Club and the Swiss Alpine Club welcome visiting members.
  • Expeditions: Reputable operators and club expeditions offer routes into the greater ranges.

Guidelines

Know the rules before you reach the trailhead.

01
About This Website

Alpine Ascents is an educational guide to mountaineering. It brings together the history of the sport, the Seven Summits, training schools, courses by level (foundational, alpine skills, technical and professional guide certification), essential skills, gear by system, sheltering, hazards and clubs, plus a Latest page for news, conditions and dates. Everything here is for information and learning only.

02
Safety Disclaimer

Mountaineering is dangerous and can cause serious injury or death. Nothing on this site replaces professional training, a qualified guide or your own judgement. Course details, prices and dates are indicative only — always confirm them with the provider, and always check current conditions and climb within your abilities.

03
Accuracy of Information

We work to keep information accurate and up to date, but we cannot guarantee it is complete or current. Heights, records, routes, course fees, event dates and conditions all change, so verify important details with official sources and the latest forecasts.

04
Using the Content

You may read, share and link to pages for personal and educational use. Please credit Alpine Ascents when you share or quote our content. Do not copy, sell or republish it commercially without permission.

05
Community Conduct

If you contact us or take part in any community features, please be respectful and honest. Abusive, misleading or harmful messages will not be accepted.

06
Changes to These Guidelines

We may update these guidelines from time to time. The latest version will always be on this page, so please check back occasionally.

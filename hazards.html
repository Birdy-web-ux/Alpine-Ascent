MOUNTAIN HAZAREDS

Over view

Mountain hazards are usually grouped as objective hazards, which exist regardless of your skill,
and subjective hazards, which come from your decisions, fitness, and preparation. Here they are
sorted by how dangerous they tend to be.

Category 1: Highest danger (often fatal, fast onset)

  • Avalanches:The leading killer in winter backcountry travel. Most victims trigger the slide
    themselves. Burial survival drops sharply after about 15 minutes.
  • Falls (slips, cliffs, scrambling, climbing):The most common cause of mountaineering
    deaths. Even short falls on steep, icy, or exposed terrain can be fatal.
  • Rockfall and icefall:Unpredictable and impossible to outrun at close range. Risk rises with
    afternoon warming, rain, and freeze-thaw cycles.
  • Lightning:Strikes exposed ridges and summits, mostly in summer afternoons. Descend
    before storms build.
  • Severe altitude illness (HAPE and HACE):Fluid in the lungs or swelling of the brain,
    usually above about 3,000 m (10,000 ft). Both are fatal without immediate descent.

Category 2: High danger (serious, often preventable with planning)

  • Hypothermia:Caused by wind, wet clothing, and exhaustion, and it can happen even in
    above-freezing temperatures. Confusion and clumsiness are early signs.
  • Sudden weather changes: Whiteouts, high winds, and rapid temperature drops lead to
    disorientation and exposure.
  • Crevasses and glaciers:Snow bridges hide deep gaps. Travel roped, with rescue skills.
  • River crossings and flash floods:Snowmelt-fed rivers rise through the day, and canyons and
    gullies can flood from storms far upstream.
  • Cornices and snow bridges:Overhanging snow collapses under weight, especially near ridgelines.
  • Getting lost or benighted:Not deadly alone, but it compounds every other hazard.

Category 3: Moderate danger (painful or disabling, rarely fatal on their own)

  • Frostbite:Mostly affects fingers, toes, nose, and ears. Risk rises with wind chill and poor
    circulation.
  • Acute mountain sickness (mild to moderate):Headache, nausea, and poor sleep. It becomes
    dangerous only if ignored while you keep climbing.
  • Dehydration and exhaustion:Dry air and exertion hide fluid loss, and fatigue leads to bad
    decisions.
  • Sun exposure and snow blindness:UV is stronger at altitude and reflects off snow.
  • Sprains, fractures, and injuries far from help:Serious mainly because of slow evacuation.
  • Dangerous wildlife:Bears, mountain lions, and moose, depending on region. Encounters are rare, and food storage and awareness help.

Category 4: Lower danger (mostly nuisances, but they can escalate)

  • Blisters and minor cuts, which can limit mobility or become infected
  • Blisters and minor cuts, which can limit mobility or become infected
  • Poisonous plants, snakes, and spiders
  • Gear failure, such as a broken pole, a lost glove, or a dead headlamp

Reducing risk across all categories

  • Check avalanche bulletins and mountain forecasts, and set turnaround times.
  • Ascend gradually, and descend at the first signs of serious altitude illness.
  • Carry layers, a map and compass or GPS, a headlamp, a first aid kit, and an emergency
    shelter.
  • Carry a beacon, probe, and shovel in avalanche terrain, and know how to use them.
  • Tell someone your route and return time, and consider a satellite messenger.
  • Take courses in first aid, avalanche safety, and navigation.
